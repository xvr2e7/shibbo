01 / 04 Lead design · 2025

Pair

Shared money, without the joint-account fiction.

A neobank wanted couples on one account. The couples didn’t. I designed a shared view of commitments that leaves personal spending private.

  • +36%Shared goals finished
  • −50%Spend disputes in support

I’d still refuse the confetti. Money between two people is already emotional.

Read the notes Hide the notes

The first prototype assumed a 50/50 split. Eighteen interviews said otherwise: one salary, shared rent, a holiday neither person wanted to itemize, and a private life that should stay private. We threw out the joint account and designed three models. The one that shipped shows commitments, never a partner’s personal balance.

I wrote the empty states with the support lead, who kept a folder of arguments that began with a push notification. No confetti, no “you crushed it.” Shared-goal completion rose 36% in the quarter after launch. Tickets about who spent what fell by half.

02 / 04 Lead design · 2024

Margin

A long document that can hold more than one author.

Researchers were co-writing in a tool built for chat. I rebuilt the product around the page, and anchored comments to sentences instead of a feed.

  • −22%Time to a shared first draft
  • 03Labs prototyping with real papers

Anchor the note to the sentence. A feed is where arguments go to disappear.

Read the notes Hide the notes

The old product organized itself like an inbox, so the paper disappeared under the conversation about the paper. I moved the document back to the center. Comments pin to a sentence and collapse when they’re resolved. The sidebar is a table of contents, not a chat.

We prototyped with real drafts from three labs — messy, cited, too long. The type scale had to hold eight thousand words without going gray. Median time to a shared first draft dropped 22%, and teams stopped exporting to a word processor to finish.

03 / 04 Lead design · 2024

Front Desk

A benefits form for a tired person on a phone.

Fourteen steps, written for caseworkers, completed by almost no one. I cut it to four decisions and specified the screen-reader path as carefully as the visual one.

  • 2.4×Completion on the main benefit
  • 04Decisions, down from fourteen

Read every question out loud to someone tired. If you feel clever, rewrite it.

Read the notes Hide the notes

I sat with applicants at a library branch and watched a 14-step form fail quietly. People were on phones. Some used screen readers. Most were anxious and short on time. We cut the application to four decisions, wrote it at a sixth-grade reading level without talking down, and made save-and-return actually save.

Focus order, error summaries, and the unhappy paths were specified, not left for QA to discover. Completion on the primary benefit rose 2.4×. Two other departments reused the pattern. The flow meets WCAG 2.2 AA.

04 / 04 Brand & product · 2023

Grove

Groceries with the nerve to edit.

The subscription felt like a warehouse. I led brand and product together, and replaced the infinite aisle with a weekly edit of twenty-four things.

  • 61%Still ordering at month three
  • 24Items, on purpose

A smaller catalog was the feature.

Read the notes Hide the notes

The founders had a catalog of four hundred items and the feeling of a loading dock. A good greengrocer edits. I led the brand and the product together so the app, the box, and the Tuesday email sounded like one person. The weekly edit holds twenty-four things. The swap flow is for people who hate fennel, and it is faster than skipping the week.

Month-three repeat rate was 61%. Skip stopped being the main action. Swaps became it. The packing slip got the same type and the same voice as checkout.

About

I stay for the second version.

The unglamorous middle of a product is the product.

I’m Elena, a product designer working on my own from Lisbon. I started in studios in Milan and London, then spent four years as a design lead at a payments company — long enough to care about ledgers, edge cases, and the people who answer the tickets.

I take one project at a time and I prefer to stay from the first interviews through the release, and the awkward week after. The teams I work best with already have a point of view. They want a designer who will argue, and still ship.

Approach

Four movements, not a process poster.

Engagements run three to six months. I don’t run a squad, and I don’t hand you a slide and leave.

  1. 01

    Discover

    I start with the constraint, not the canvas. Interviews, old tickets, and a walkthrough with someone who uses the product on a bad day.

  2. 02

    Shape

    A few flows, a prototype sturdy enough to argue with, and principles the team can reuse when I’m not in the room.

  3. 03

    Build

    Specs an engineer doesn’t have to decode. I pair on the hard states: empty, error, slow, and the one nobody designed.

  4. 04

    Stay

    After launch I watch the numbers we agreed mattered, and I design the next cut while the first one is still warm.
