About

I'm a hydrologist based in Portland, Oregon, where the Willamette meets the Columbia and the rain never quite stops. For twenty years I've studied how rivers move, flood, dry up, and reshape the land they cross. I hold a Ph.D. in fluvial geomorphology from UC Davis and currently lead watershed research at the Pacific Northwest Water Institute.

Somewhere along the way, the data wasn't enough. I started writing essays — about what rivers remember, about the politics of dams, about standing knee-deep in glacial meltwater at 4 a.m. These pieces live at the intersection of science, memory, and landscape. They've appeared in Orion, High Country News, Aeon, and The Yale Review.

When I'm not measuring discharge or writing sentences, I'm paddling something — canoe, kayak, or just my own two feet across a shin-deep ford.

Essays

  • March 2024 Orion

    The River That Flows Backward

    On the Chicago River's reversal, and what it means to force a body of water to forget its own direction. An essay about engineering hubris, sewage, and the strange grief of undone geography.

  • November 2023 Aeon

    Meander: In Praise of the Indirect Path

    Why rivers don't run straight, and why we shouldn't either. A meditation on sinuosity, patience, and the physics of erosion as a model for creative thought.

  • July 2023 High Country News

    What the Salmon Know

    Following Chinook upstream through four dams on the Snake River. A field report that became an elegy — for fish, for free-flowing water, for the Columbia Basin's broken promise.

  • February 2023 The Yale Review

    A Hydrology of Grief

    My mother kept a rain gauge in her garden for thirty-one years. After she died, I inherited the notebooks. This essay follows water from her backyard to the nearest creek and out to the Pacific.

  • September 2022 Orion

    The Floodplain Journals

    Three years of notes from a restored wetland on the Willamette. Beavers returned first, then the herons, then something harder to measure — a feeling that the land was breathing again.

  • April 2022 Emergence Magazine

    Confluence: Where Rivers Meet and Cultures Collide

    Every great city sits at a confluence. I visited seven of them — Khartoum, Pittsburgh, Lyon, Manaus, Allahabad, Koblenz, and Belgrade — to ask what happens when two currents merge into one.

  • October 2021 Aeon

    The Disappeared: Rivers Lost to Concrete

    Beneath London, Seoul, and Los Angeles, buried rivers still flow in culverts and tunnels. An investigation into what we lose when we pave over living water — and the growing movement to bring it back.

  • March 2021 High Country News

    Drought as Teacher

    In the summer of 2020, I watched my study creek disappear. The bed cracked, the willows browned, the crayfish buried themselves in mud. What a dry riverbed teaches a hydrologist about letting go.

  • August 2020 Orion

    Riffle, Pool, Run

    A glossary of river forms as autobiography. Each term — eddy, oxbow, braided channel — mapped onto a moment from a life spent wading into cold water with a clipboard and a sense of wonder.
