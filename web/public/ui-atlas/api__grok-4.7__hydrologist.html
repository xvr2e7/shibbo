Essays about rivers

Mara Elling measures rivers and writes about what the numbers leave out. She trained as a hydrologist and spent eleven years with a western water agency — stage, discharge, sediment, snow — first on the Clark Fork, later on coastal streams that rise and fall inside a single storm.

Since 2021 she has written full time, and still keeps a notebook and a current meter in the truck. She lives in Corvallis. The pieces here are field notes in longer form, not agency records.

Currently — late-summer low flow on a Willamette tributary, before the rains.

  1. 01 Stage and Sentence Clark Fork at the county bridge · 2025 · 14 min A staff gauge is a ruler someone trusted enough to bolt to a bridge.

    It reads in tenths, which is a politeness. Water does not arrive in tenths. On the morning I keep returning to, stage had climbed through the night while the town did ordinary things, and by six the lower trail was a rumor. I wrote the number in the book, then stood longer than the method requires, listening to the piers take the current. The essay is about that extra minute, and about the sentence a hydrograph almost is.

  2. 02 Silt, Kept Missouri River bottoms · 2024 · 11 min After the water left, the fields were a different color two pastures inland.

    We walked while the ground was still too soft for a truck. The mud held the river’s brown, which was not the soil’s. I took a core with a length of pipe. In eight inches there were three floods, if you knew how to be wrong carefully. The farmer wanted to know when he could plant. I wanted the middle layer’s year. We were both asking the silt to keep a promise it had already kept.

  3. 03 Baseflow in January A spring creek above the Bitterroot · 2024 · 9 min Most of a river in midwinter is not weather. It is groundwater paying an old debt.

    From the road the creek looks still. Under the shelf ice it moves at the speed of a long decision, fed by gravel and last year’s snow and a spring no one has named. The flow was too small for the meter I trust, so I used a bucket and a watch. January rivers are mostly invisible work. This one was paying out, clear and cold, as if weather were a rumor from another valley.

  4. 04 Reading Braids Platte River · 2023 · 16 min A channel that will not stay the line we drew for it in 1954.

    On the Platte the river is several rivers, and they trade places while you eat lunch. A thread that was wadable at nine is a chute at noon. I had a map in a plastic sleeve. It was not wrong so much as retired. Cottonwood seedlings had voted, densely, for a channel the map did not recognize. I stopped trying to say which braid was the main one. The river was not taking the question seriously.

  5. 05 Ice-Out Outlet of a northern lake · 2023 · 8 min There is a morning when the river changes state. The sound comes back before the color.

    A tick, then a long sliding note, then the open-water smell, which is metal and leaf-rot and cold. I had come for a stage reading and stayed for the change of state. By afternoon the outlet was a river again in the ordinary sense: moving, dark, willing to take a stick and not give it back. The ice stayed piled on the bend like a decision someone had overturned.

  6. 06 A Gauge in Flood Clark Fork, county bridge · 2022 · 12 min The instrument is calm for as long as the bank holds. This is about the hour it didn’t.

    There is a procedure for when the river takes the bank the gauge is bolted to. I had read it. I had not stood in it. The recorder kept a calm account of a situation that was no longer the situation, and for an hour our published number described a river that had moved sideways into the cottonwoods. We flagged the data. We did not, that day, find a sentence for the gap. This is a late attempt.

  7. 07 Color of Melt A glacial stream, Canadian Rockies · 2021 · 10 min Rock ground to flour. A gray-green that photographs as nothing.

    Glacial flour hangs in the water and turns it a color cameras flatten. In a bottle it is green and luminous, milk lit from inside, and it settles slowly enough that you can watch an hour disappear. The stream was colder than the air by a margin that felt personal. I wrote “gray-green” in the notebook and knew I had failed the color, which was the point of trying.

  8. 08 Banks We Invent Riprap, lines, and edges · 2020 · 13 min A river has a bank only in the sense that a conversation does: provisionally, and for someone.

    We harden the agreement with rock, then act surprised when it moves downstream and signs itself on someone else’s land. I have walked riprap already being edited — stones sorted by a high flow, the fabric showing through like a bad alibi. Property lines make even less impression. This is not an argument against banks. It is a note on their authorship. We write them. The river revises.
