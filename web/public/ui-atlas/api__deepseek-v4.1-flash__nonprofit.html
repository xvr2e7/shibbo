The problem is uneven

Some blocks get shade.
Most don't.

In nearly every American city, the neighborhoods with the least money and the most pavement are also the hottest. A tree planted on the right block is one of the cheapest, most durable pieces of public infrastructure a city can own — and it keeps paying back for eighty years.

Shade that actually cools

Blocks without canopy can run 7°F hotter than leafy ones a mile away. Mature street trees drop surface temperatures, cut air-conditioning bills, and make walking to the bus stop bearable in July.

Air you can breathe

Leaves catch fine particulates, soak up stormwater before it floods basements, and pull carbon out of the sky. Neighborhoods with more canopy report measurably lower asthma rates.

Work, close to home

We hire and train local planters, then pay residents a small stipend to water their own block for the first three summers. The tree survives because the neighborhood owns it.

How we work

Plant. Water. Hand it over.

Most urban tree programs fail at year two — the sapling gets planted, everyone celebrates, and then nobody waters it. Our whole model is built around that second summer.

01

Plant

Crews work with neighbors, one block at a time. Species are chosen for the street itself — drought-tolerant, mostly native, and big enough at maturity to cast real shade over a sidewalk.

Average 42 trees per block
02

Care

Trees die without water. We install mulch rings and drip lines, then run a text-reminder watering crew through the first three summers — the window where nearly all losses happen.

92% survive to year three
03

Teach

Free tree-steward training, schoolyard orchards, and paid youth internships in every city we work in. When we leave, there's a trained person on the block who knows how to prune.

Where we're planting

Shade equity, mapped.

We only start in a neighborhood if it's hotter than the city median and has less than 10% canopy cover. Then we stay for a decade.

Priority blocks are 5.4°F hotter on average

That's the gap between a block with mature street trees and one without. It's also, almost exactly, the gap between the wealthiest and poorest census tracts in the same city.
