A day in the valley

Camille & Jonah

A Saturday that started in fog and ended with the windows open. They were married under a maple that had already turned, with forty people and a dog who refused to sit.

I photographed them the way they actually are with each other: interrupting, laughing, fixing a collar. The formal portraits took eleven minutes. The rest of the day took care of itself.

02 — The approach

I stay out of the way, and pay attention.

I started photographing friends’ weddings in borrowed apartments, with one camera and no assistant. I still prefer to work that way: small, early, and mostly invisible.

I don’t arrive with a list of sixty mandatory poses. If that is the day you want directed, I am the wrong photographer, and I will tell you on our call. Couples hire me when they want the day to look like itself. A little weather. A little mess. The exact face your mother made.

We talk beforehand — who matters, what you’re worried about, what you hope to remember in twenty years — and then I follow the light and the people.

05 — Studio notes

A few things I believe.

On timelines

Why I leave the schedule porous

Most timelines are written to soothe anxiety, not to describe a day. I ask for the three things that cannot move — a ceremony, a meal, a last song — and leave the rest open. The photographs you will love are usually hiding in the open parts.

On arrival

Photograph the room before it fills

Empty chairs. A jacket on the wrong chair. The light on a wall at 3:40, which will not come back. I arrive early for these, and they often become the pictures that hold the rest in place.

On materials

Film is not a personality

I use film when the day wants it, not as a costume. Digital lets me stay with a moment longer. Neither is more honest. The honesty is in what I choose to keep, and in not sanding a face down until it belongs to no one.
