The gap

Shade is uneven. We can point to the blocks.

A street tree is public infrastructure. It cools the sidewalk, slows stormwater, and makes a bus stop bearable in July. In a lot of cities that infrastructure landed on the leafier blocks and skipped the rest.

  • Bare asphalt holds the day’s heat and gives it back all evening.
  • Canopy still tracks old lines of investment. We start where it is thinnest.
  • A tree only cools the block if it is alive in year three.

Method

How a block gets shade.

We plant in the public right-of-way, with city permission. Not in secret, and not as a photo that nobody waters.

  1. 01

    Find the hot blocks

    Canopy maps, bus stops, and walks with people who already live there. Priority goes to thin shade, hard summers, and a pit that will not wreck the sidewalk.

  2. 02

    Plant with the block

    Species follow the wires, the salt, the soil, and the width of the pit. Neighbors set the trees. We do not plant a tree the block did not ask for.

  3. 03

    Stay for three summers

    Water, mulch, stakes, and a phone number that gets answered. Survival is the metric. A photo on planting day is not.

  1. Day 0Wide pit, decent soil, the right species, neighbors on the shovels.
  2. Summer 1Weekly water. The year most saplings die if nobody comes back.
  3. Summer 2Mulch, stake check, prune only what the tree actually needs.
  4. Summer 3Handoff. The block knows the tree. We stay on call.

Species

Trees we trust in a pit.

Not a catalog. A short list matched to pits, wires, wet corners, and July heat. The site decides, not the prettiest photo.

Swamp white oak

Quercus bicolor

Long-lived shade where the pit is wide enough. Calmer about wet, compacted soil than fussier oaks.

Thornless honeylocust

Gleditsia triacanthos var. inermis

Filtered light, not a solid ceiling. A tough tree where a full oak would be too much tree.

Bald cypress

Taxodium distichum

For low corners that hold water. Feathers in summer, copper in fall. Knees show up mainly if the site stays wet.

Allegheny serviceberry

Amelanchier laevis

Under the wires. Spring flowers, fruit for birds, a size that will not pick a fight with the lines.

Kentucky coffeetree

Gymnocladus dioicus

Open crown, late to leaf out, very tough. Male cultivars where pods would pile up on the walk.

Black gum

Nyssa sylvatica

A steady street tree with a fall color people cross the road for. Less overused than the usual maple.

FAQ

Questions from the sidewalk.

Can you plant a tree in my yard?

No. We plant in the public right-of-way — the strip between sidewalk and curb — and only with city permission. For a yard tree, a working version of this desk would point you to a local yard-tree program.

Who owns the tree afterward?

Usually the city. We steward the early years with neighbors. After the handoff, the block knows how to water in a drought, and the tree is on the municipal map.

Why not just the cheapest species?

Wires, pit width, salt, and soil decide. A bargain tree in the wrong pit is a dead tree and a broken sidewalk. We would rather plant fewer and keep them.

Will this page take my money?

No. Blockshade is a concept website, not a registered charity. Forms do not charge a card, reserve a tree, or send email.

A sapling is a promise, not a photo. The work that matters is the third July, when someone still shows up with a hose.
