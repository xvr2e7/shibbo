Field notes & observations

01 A river is never just a riverOn learning to see a watershed as a living whole.Field notes   /   6 min read +

Stand beside a river long enough and the edges begin to blur. The water in front of you has been cloud and snow, groundwater and rain. The banks are held together by roots; the current carries pieces of the hills downstream.

A watershed is all the land that gathers water toward a common place. It is also a useful way to practice attention: to understand that what happens uphill eventually finds its way down.

02 The shape of a slow seasonWhat low water can teach us about paying attention.Reflections   /   5 min read +

In late summer, the river reveals its architecture. Gravel bars emerge, dark stones warm in the sun, and channels that seemed continuous become a string of separate pools.

Low water is not an absence of a river. It is another expression of one, asking us to look carefully at the places where life waits for the rain.

03 Where the river remembersFloodplains, old maps, and the paths water finds again.Landscape   /   8 min read +

On an old map, a river’s former bends look like handwriting. Some have been cut off by roads or levees; others linger as wetlands, visible only when the light is right.

Floodplains are not mistakes in the landscape. They are part of a river’s room to move, a record of where water has been and a reminder of where it may go again.

04 Listening at the water’s edgeA small case for spending more time beside a stream.Field notes   /   4 min read +

Before taking a measurement, I like to sit for a while. There is the sound of water over stone, of course, but also the rustle of willow leaves and the occasional splash that gives away a fish.

Numbers help us understand a place. So does returning to it, in different weather and different seasons, until its changes become familiar.
