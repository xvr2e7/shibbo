FIELD REPORT NO. 07 // FEATURED ESSAY 36.8851° N, 119.7732° W

The Ghost of Tulare Lake

For a century, the largest freshwater body west of the Mississippi was erased by surveyor lines, cotton levees, and groundwater pumps. Then came the atmospheric rivers of 2023—and the basin remembered its lowest contour.

Published in Places Journal • Spring 2024 • 14 min read

Topography has a longer memory than property law. Stand on a levee road south of Corcoran, California, in a dry year, and you see an industrial grid oflaser-leveled laser-planed soil stretching toward the haze of the Kettleman Hills. The ditches run at ninety-degree angles. Water moves only where diesel pumps and concrete turnouts instruct it to move. Yet beneath the safflower and pistachio rows lies a clay lacustrine lens—fine, gray, and virtually impermeable—deposited over millennia by the Kings, Kaweah, Tule, and Kern rivers.

To a hydrologist, a lake is not merely water; it is a closed depression in the gravitational field. You can divert the inflows and name the lakebed after nineteenth-century land barons, but you cannot repeal gravity. When the Sierra Nevada snowpack reached 260 percent of normal in the spring of 2023, the water did not consult county parcel maps. It simply sought the thalweg of the old sloughs.

FIG 1.4 — Subsidence Bowl Anomaly: Between 1925 and 2022, deep aquifer pumping caused parts of the Tulare basin floor to sink by up to 28 feet, subtly shifting the lowest point of the historical lakebed four miles southeast.

Standing at the new shoreline in May, listening to white pelicans return to a body of water that had not existed in their lifetimes, I was struck by the hubris of the word reclamation. We never reclaimed the valley from the water; the water merely granted us a hundred-year lease, and the terms were always written in snow-water equivalent.

NO. 06 Atchafalaya Basin, LA Autumn 2024 30.9803° N, 91.6634° W

Avulsion: Notes on a River Changing Its Mind

Every thousand years, the lower Mississippi abandons its delta for a steeper, shorter path to the Gulf. At the Old River Control Structure, we spend millions of tons of limestone and steel trying to talk a continent out of its next jump.

Orion Magazine • 18 min read

A river builds its own prison. Every spring flood spills over the banks, dropping coarse sands immediately adjacent to the channel to form natural levees, while slowly raising the elevation of the riverbed itself. Eventually, the river is perched above its own floodplain—flowing inside an elevated trough of its own making, looking down at steeper, more inviting gradients on either side.

Geomorphologists call the breaking point an avulsion. The Atchafalaya offers the Mississippi a path to the sea that is 145 miles shorter and twice as steep as the current route past New Orleans. Only a line of steel tainter gates along Louisiana Highway 15 keeps the seventy-percent/thirty-percent legal truce intact. Walking the vibrating catwalk during high water, you can feel the shear stress through the soles of your boots: a river leaning all its weight against a locked door.

NO. 05 Elwha & Klamath Rivers Winter 2023 48.0954° N, 123.5621° W

The Sound of Bedload

What happens acoustically on the floor of a canyon in the seventy-two hours after a century-old concrete dam is blasted open? Listening to cobbles move for the first time since 1912 with underwater hydrophones.

Emergence Magazine • 11 min read

We think of rivers as liquid, so we listen to their surfaces: the white-noise hiss of riffles, the low gulp of an eddy line. But below a certain velocity threshold, the real work of a mountain river happens along its bed, where granite cobbles the size of loaves of bread roll, saltate, and strike one another in the dark.

When we lowered a calibrated hydrophone into the lower Elwha six months after the Glines Canyon Dam came down, the headphones didn't transmit water sounds. They transmitted the sound of a bowling alley inside a cathedral—millions of tons of starved gravel finally marching seaward to rebuild a vanishing coast.

NO. 04 Willamette River, OR Summer 2022 44.4921° N, 123.2491° W

Reading the LiDAR Bare-Earth

When airborne lasers strip away the Douglas firs and hazelnut orchards of the Willamette Valley, the ground reveals a swirling calligraphy of oxbows, scrolls, and braided scars left over from the Missoula Floods.

Cabinet Magazine • 9 min read

In 1851, the General Land Office surveyors complained incessantly in their leather-bound field books about the Willamette: it wasn't one river, they wrote, but four or five simultaneous channels tangled in drift logs two hundred feet long. Over the next eighty years, the Army Corps of Engineers pulled 85,000 snags from the river and pinned its banks with riprap, forcing a multi-thread anastomosing system into a single polite canal.

Yet when you load a 1-meter LiDAR digital elevation model and stretch the vertical color ramp across just six feet of relief, the nineteenth-century river leaps off the screen. The ghostly braids are still there, holding three inches of winter rainwater in the middle of a ryegrass field.

NO. 03 Colorado River Basin Autumn 2021 38.9831° N, 110.1494° W

A Taxonomy of Zero-Flow Days

How do you calibrate a stream gauge when the river turns to dry alkali crust for four months of the year? A portrait of the technicians who measure diminishing baseflows at the edges of the 1922 Colorado River Compact.

High Country News • 15 min read

In hydrological notation, zero is the hardest number to verify. A pressure transducer inside a corrugated steel stilling well can tell you when the water drops below the intake pipe, but it cannot distinguish between a dry sandy riffle and a disconnected pool where three flannelmouth suckers are waiting out August under a cutbank.

The 1922 Compact divided 16.5 million acre-feet of water based on one of the wettest pluvial decades in five hundred years of tree-ring records. Today, our gauges measure the gap between political fiction and the physics of vapor-pressure deficit.

NO. 02 Thames & Fleet, London / Mill Creek, PA Spring 2020 39.9581° N, 75.2148° W

Brick Arch, Buried Creek

In the nineteenth century, growing cities solved the problem of polluted urban streams by bricking them into subterranean sewers. Now, slumping rowhouses and flooded basements trace the exact meanders of buried watersheds.

Places Journal • 12 min read

Walk diagonally across the grid of West Philadelphia and watch the porch steps. For four blocks the brick rowhouses sit level and plumb; then, along a curving swath fifty yards wide, lintels tilt, sidewalks buckle, and vacant lots bloom with goldenrod and ailanthus. You are walking across the floodplain of Mill Creek, which was encased in a twenty-foot brick arch in the 1880s and buried under thirty feet of coal ash fill.

NO. 01 Tagliamento River, Italy Autumn 2019 46.2089° N, 12.9765° E

In Praise of the Reference Reach

Every ecological restoration needs a baseline. Why river scientists from around the world travel to a mile-wide corridor of white limestone gravel in northeastern Italy—the last wild braided alpine river in Europe.

Aeon • 13 min read

Most Europeans and Americans have never seen an adult river. We have seen rivers placed in straightjackets between agricultural dikes, rivers starved of wood, rivers whose floodplains have been amputated. Studying only channelized rivers to understand fluvial mechanics is like studying zoo animals to understand migration. Then you drive onto the Pinzano bridge above the Tagliamento, and the sheer, unruly width of its gravel bars steals your breath.
