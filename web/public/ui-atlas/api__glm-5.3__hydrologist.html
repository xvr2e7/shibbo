NEWEST FIRST, AS SEDIMENT ACCUMULATES

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    Discharge is a polite word for a violent fact: the volume of water passing a fixed point, every second, without pause or apology. We compute it as width times depth times velocity, which sounds like homework and feels, in January, like arithmetic performed from inside a thing that is trying to take your legs out from under you.

    The current meter does not care about any of this. It counts propeller turns the way a stubborn witness counts facts — one, then another, then another — and leaves the believing to you. What it knows, and what no spreadsheet ever will, is that a river is not a quantity. It is a behavior. The number in the logbook is the river's mood, translated at one place in one moment, and out of date before the ink dries.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    The orchard had been abandoned for years when the November flood found it — four feet of grey water sliding between the rows, patient as bookkeeping. When the water withdrew, it left behind two acres of ground that had not existed in October: new land, fine as flour, holding the print of a heron's foot like a fossil holding its own future.

    This is what a flood actually is, though we seldom say so: a transfer. Dirt moves from the hillside to the orchard; the orchard, some later century, will move on toward the sea. The ledger never closes. Every river I have ever measured was carrying somebody's field downstream, a handful at a time, and calling it sediment so that nobody had to cry.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    We are taught early to distrust water we cannot see through, which is a decent rule for a swimming hole and a slander to apply to a river. Clear water is idle water — water that has recently done nothing. The muddy river is not hiding anything. It has been up in the hills. It has been rearranging things. It is carrying the mountains to the sea, a grain at a time.

    Turbidity, in other words, is honesty. When Cascara Creek runs chocolate in March, it is telling you exactly what it did last week, and where, and how hard it rained. The clear river tells you almost nothing at all. I have come to trust muddy water the way I trust people who talk with their hands: you can see what they mean.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    For three winters I kept the night shift at the lower station, which sounds lonely and mostly was. The log wants numbers — stage at midnight, at two, at four. It does not ask what the river sounds like at two in the morning in December, and I never found a column for that.

    People assume the trouble with night work is staying awake. It isn't. The trouble is that you start to hear the river change — a new riffle building over the gravel bar, the pitch of the water thickening as rain arrives from upstream, hours before any instrument agrees. There is no one to tell at two in the morning. So I wrote it down where the numbers wouldn't mind: in the margins, and then, eventually, here.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    Every map is a portrait of a river in a moment when the river happened to be in a good mood. The real river is elsewhere — behind the levee, remembering when the map was drawn, and considering its options. A meandering river does not cross a valley. It edits one.

    Stand on a bridge long enough and you can watch the revision happen: the outside of the bend scoured deep, the inside fattening with borrowed ground, the current filing slowly toward the farmhouse. The oxbow lakes scattered across any floodplain are not ruins. They are drafts — the river's discarded sentences, lying in the grass, still legible.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    Ice does not leave a river the way it leaves a glass. It lets go — all at once, with an opinion. One morning in March the channel that had sat silent since December began to groan like a door in an old house, and by noon it was moving: plates the size of pool tables grinding south, the whole river shrugging off the season in an afternoon.

    The danger, the locals will tell you, is the jam — ice piling against a bend until the water behind it stands up and starts looking around for somewhere else to be. What I remember instead is the sound afterward: the strange loud silence of open water where silence had been, every gauge on the creek reading a little high, as if the river too was relieved to have gotten away with it.

  • FROM THE ESSAY

    The first instrument I ever learned was a painted board bolted to a bridge pier — a staff gauge, graduated in tenths, reliable as gravity and about as talkative. Walt, who had thirty years on the gauge before me, handed me the binoculars on my first morning and told me to call out the number. Two point four, I said. He wrote it down, and said: now say it like you're sure.

    It took me a year to understand what he meant. The number was never just the number — it was yesterday's number, plus rain, plus snowmelt, plus whatever the beavers were doing a mile upstream. Everything on this site is an attempt at that kind of certainty: essays about rivers by someone whose whole trade is admitting, nightly, exactly how deep the water is. The rest is marginalia.

About

TWISP, WASHINGTON
48.3657° N · 120.1131° W

I measure rivers for a living — stage, discharge, the slow arithmetic of sediment. For fourteen years I have worked the streams of the upper Methow Valley: wading winter channels with a current meter, coaxing gauges through ice, reading the valley's diary one flood at a time.

I came to hydrology through a summer job counting salmon redds, and to essays through the discovery that the numbers kept leaving things out — the color of a February river, the sound ice makes when it lets go. The writing is my attempt to put those back. It is filed here, next to the data, in the hope that the two might argue.

"The log wants numbers. It never asks what the river sounds like at two in the morning, in December." — NIGHTS AT THE GAUGING STATION, Nº 04

I live in Twisp with a hydrologically indifferent dog, a drawer of obsolete survey maps, and a rain gauge that over-reads because it sits too near the fence. New essays appear when the river and my calendar allow.
